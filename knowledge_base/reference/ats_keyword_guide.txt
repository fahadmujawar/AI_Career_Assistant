APPLICANT TRACKING SYSTEMS AND KEYWORD SCREENING

An applicant tracking system (ATS) is the software an employer uses to receive,
store, search and manage job applications. Common examples include Workday,
Taleo, SuccessFactors, Greenhouse, Lever and iCIMS. An ATS is primarily a
database and workflow tool. It is widely but incorrectly believed that these
systems automatically reject most applications on the basis of a keyword score.
In practice, most rejection decisions are made by a recruiter reading a
shortlist, and the ATS influences the outcome mainly by determining whether an
application appears in that shortlist at all.

PARSING
When a CV is submitted, the ATS extracts its text and attempts to map content
into structured fields such as name, contact details, employment history with
dates, education and skills. Parsing accuracy depends on document structure.
Multi-column layouts, text placed inside tables or text boxes, headers and
footers, graphics, icons and skill-rating bars are frequently misread or
dropped, because the extracted reading order does not match the visual order.
Text stored as an image cannot be extracted at all. Non-standard section
headings such as "Where I Have Worked" may not be recognised as employment
history. A single-column layout with conventional headings - Professional
Summary, Skills, Experience, Education - parses most reliably. Modern systems
handle PDF files produced from a word processor without difficulty, but a PDF
created by scanning or exporting an image will fail.

HOW RECRUITERS ACTUALLY SEARCH
Recruiters typically search the candidate database using keywords and boolean
queries for the skills that matter most to a role, for example a query
combining Python with PyTorch and a location. Candidates whose CVs do not
contain the searched terms do not appear in the result set, regardless of how
well qualified they are. This is the main mechanism by which a strong candidate
is never seen. Keywords must therefore appear in the exact form a recruiter is
likely to type. Because usage varies, it is safer to include both the acronym
and the expanded phrase on first use, for example "retrieval-augmented
generation (RAG)" or "large language model (LLM)". A CV that describes work
only in general terms, without naming the specific tools, frameworks and
techniques used, is effectively invisible to keyword search.

HARD FILTERS
Many application forms include structured knockout questions covering years of
experience, education level, work authorisation, location and language. Answers
to these are structured data rather than free text and are commonly used to
filter automatically. Unlike keyword matching, these filters are absolute: a
posting requiring four to six years of experience will exclude a candidate with
none, and no amount of CV optimisation changes that outcome. Distinguishing
between a keyword problem, which can be fixed by rewriting, and an experience
or eligibility problem, which cannot, is essential when deciding whether an
application is worth submitting.

TAILORING
Because both keyword search and recruiter reading are role-specific, a single
generic CV performs poorly across different role families. Effective tailoring
means adopting the vocabulary of the posting where it genuinely describes work
already done, ensuring the required tools and techniques are named explicitly,
and removing content aimed at a different role family. Tailoring does not mean
claiming experience that does not exist, and does not mean concealed keyword
stuffing such as invisible white text, which is easily detected and treated as
dishonesty.

QUANTIFICATION AND EVIDENCE
Recruiters reading a shortlist spend a short time on each CV. Bullets that name
a tool, a scale and an outcome are assessed faster and more favourably than
abstract descriptions of responsibility. Statements that cannot be quantified
or evidenced tend to be read as padding, and a CV composed largely of such
statements suggests the underlying work was limited even when it was not.
