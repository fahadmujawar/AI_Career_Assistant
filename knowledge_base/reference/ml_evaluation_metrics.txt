EVALUATION METRICS FOR MACHINE LEARNING SYSTEMS

Evaluation is the process of measuring whether a model does what it is
intended to do, using quantitative measures computed against known or
reference data. A model without an evaluation procedure cannot be shown to
have improved, because there is nothing against which to compare a change.
Most job descriptions in machine learning list model evaluation as a
requirement, and the specific metrics expected depend on the task type.

CLASSIFICATION METRICS
Accuracy is the proportion of predictions that are correct. It is misleading
on imbalanced datasets, because a classifier that always predicts the majority
class can score highly while being useless. Precision is the proportion of
positive predictions that are actually positive, and recall is the proportion
of actual positives that were correctly identified. These trade off against
each other: raising a decision threshold typically increases precision and
decreases recall. The F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall,
used when a single number is needed. A confusion matrix shows the full
breakdown of correct and incorrect predictions per class. ROC-AUC measures
ranking quality across all thresholds and is threshold-independent.

IMAGE QUALITY METRICS
Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) expresses the ratio between the maximum
possible pixel value and the mean squared error between two images, on a
logarithmic decibel scale. Higher values indicate greater pixel-level
similarity. PSNR reflects only per-pixel differences and correlates poorly
with human perception, so two images that look similar can score badly if
shifted or differently exposed.

The Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) compares two images on luminance,
contrast and local structure rather than raw pixel differences, producing a
value up to 1, where 1 indicates identical images. SSIM correlates more
closely with human judgement than PSNR and is widely used in medical imaging.

Both PSNR and SSIM are reference-based metrics: they compare one image against
one specific ground-truth image, and are designed for tasks such as
reconstruction, denoising and super-resolution where a true paired reference
exists. They are not well suited to unconditional generative sampling, where
a generated image has no corresponding real image to be compared against. In
that setting, pairing generated and real images arbitrarily produces low
absolute scores that reflect the absence of correspondence rather than poor
image quality, and only relative comparisons between models evaluated
identically remain meaningful.

DISTRIBUTION-LEVEL METRICS FOR GENERATIVE MODELS
Frechet Inception Distance (FID) compares the distribution of features
extracted from real and generated image sets by a pretrained Inception
network, rather than comparing individual image pairs. Lower values indicate
that the two distributions are closer. FID is the standard metric for
unpaired image generation, though its Inception features were learned on
natural images and may not capture medical image semantics well. Kernel
Inception Distance (KID) is a related measure that is less biased on small
sample sizes. Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS) measures
perceptual distance using learned features and is used where paired
comparison is appropriate.

RETRIEVAL AND RANKING METRICS
Systems that return a ranked list of results are evaluated against a labelled
set of queries with known correct answers. Hit rate at k is the proportion of
queries for which at least one correct item appears in the top k results.
Recall at k is the proportion of all correct items that appear in the top k.
Mean reciprocal rank averages the reciprocal of the position of the first
correct result, rewarding systems that rank the correct item higher rather
than merely including it. Precision at k measures how much of the returned
set is relevant, which matters when irrelevant results consume a limited
context budget.

EVALUATING GENERATED TEXT
Language model outputs are commonly assessed on faithfulness, meaning whether
every claim in the output is supported by the source material provided, and on
relevance, meaning whether the output addresses the question asked. Because
these properties are difficult to compute directly, they are often scored by a
separate language model applied to a written rubric, an approach known as
LLM-as-judge. Judge scores are estimates and should be validated against
human labels on a sample before being trusted. Refusal behaviour is evaluated
separately: a system given questions whose answers are absent from its sources
should decline rather than answer, and a confident answer produced from the
model's own prior knowledge counts as a failure even if it happens to be
factually correct.

BASELINES AND RELATIVE IMPROVEMENT
An absolute metric value is difficult to interpret without a point of
comparison. Evaluation therefore requires a baseline, most often the
unmodified or pretrained model, measured under identical conditions on
identical data. A result that is low in absolute terms can still demonstrate
that an intervention worked if it improves clearly on that baseline. Where
absolute values are compared against figures published in the literature,
differences in dataset, resolution, sample size and metric implementation must
be accounted for, since these frequently differ enough to make direct
comparison invalid.

LIMITS OF QUANTITATIVE EVALUATION
No single metric captures whether a model is fit for its purpose. Metrics
measure specific, narrow properties, and a model can score well while failing
in ways the metric does not observe, such as a synthetic radiograph that is
numerically similar to a reference but anatomically implausible. Quantitative
evaluation is therefore usually combined with qualitative inspection and, in
regulated or high-stakes domains, expert review.
